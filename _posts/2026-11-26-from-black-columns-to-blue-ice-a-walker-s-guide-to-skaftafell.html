Skaftafell is a place to see waterfalls, mountain views, and glacier ice without leaving the marked land trails. For a broader look at the region and its guided glacier trips, visitvatnajokull.org explains the differences between visiting the national park and walking on ice.

The best-known hike leads to Svartifoss, a waterfall framed by dark basalt columns. A longer loop adds other waterfalls, a viewpoint, and an old turf house.

You can also walk toward a view of Skaftafellsjökull, an outlet glacier flowing from the larger Vatnajökull ice cap. Both walks begin in an area with a visitor center and parking.

A Small Part of a Vast National Park

Skaftafell sits inside Vatnajökull National Park, which was established in 2008 and covers roughly 14% of Iceland. The protected area stretches far beyond the trails described here.

Vatnajökull is the ice cap itself. The national park includes that ice and a wider landscape of mountains, rivers, volcanic areas, and black plains.

The Vatnajökull World Heritage listing explains how glaciers and volcanoes shape the park together. Fire can erupt beneath ice, while meltwater carries rock and sediment away.

Öræfajökull, a volcano beneath the ice, erupted in 1362 and again in 1727. The eruption in 1362 was much larger. This history gives the quiet view from Skaftafell another dimension.

Start at Skaftafellsstofa Visitor Center

Skaftafellsstofa Visitor Center is the starting point for the S2 loop and a useful place to get your bearings. Ask about the route you plan to walk and any local conditions affecting it.

The center opens year-round. Published hours run from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. in January, February, and December, and from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. in March through May and in November.

Summer brings longer hours. From June through August, the published schedule is 9 a.m. to 7 p.m. In September and October, it is 9 a.m. to 6 p.m.

Hours, fees, weather, and trail access can change. Check current information before your trip, then use the visitor center to make your final route choice.

The S2 Loop: Waterfalls, Views, and an Old Farm

The S2 Svartifoss–Sjónarsker–Sel loop covers 5.8 kilometers, or about 3.6 miles. The estimated walking time is two hours, with approximately 250 meters, or 820 feet, of ascent.

The walk begins at Skaftafellsstofa. Its named stops include Svartifoss, the Sjónarsker viewpoint, and the old turf house at Sel.

Hundafoss and Magnúsarfoss add two more waterfalls to the outing. This is a good choice if you want a varied walk rather than a quick visit to a single attraction.

Allow more than the two-hour estimate if you like to stop for photographs or spend time at Sel. The climb also makes this a more demanding choice than a short walk on level ground.

Svartifoss and Its Dark Basalt Columns

Svartifoss is the star of the S2 loop. Water drops in front of dark basalt columns that stand close together like a wall of stone.

The columns give the waterfall its sharp, orderly frame. The water is the moving part of the scene, while the black rock holds its shape behind it.

The walk to Svartifoss follows a marked land trail. It does not take you onto a glacier, and seeing the waterfall does not require glacier equipment.

Give yourself time to look at both the falls and their setting. The striking detail is not just the water, but the contrast between the bright flow and the dark columns around it.

Hundafoss and Magnúsarfoss

Svartifoss gets the most attention, but the S2 route also includes Hundafoss and Magnúsarfoss. Together, the three falls give the hike more variety than its most famous photograph suggests.

These are useful places to pause on a walk that climbs about 250 meters. A short break also gives you time to notice how each stretch of water has a different setting.

Keep the full 5.8-kilometer loop in mind when planning your pace. Stopping at the earlier waterfalls can make the day more enjoyable, but those stops add time to the two-hour walking estimate.

Bring water and a snack if you expect to linger. A walk with several natural stopping points is easier to enjoy when you are not trying to race back to the parking area.

Sjónarsker: A Place to Take in the View

 

Sjónarsker is the viewpoint named in the S2 route. It offers a reason to slow down and look beyond the path immediately in front of you.

The loop combines this outlook with Svartifoss and Sel, so the landscape changes as you walk. One moment the focus is falling water, the next it is the wider terrain.

Use the viewpoint as a natural pause during the outing. The route’s approximately 250 meters of ascent can feel different depending on your fitness and the weather.

A warm layer is worth carrying even if you feel comfortable while climbing. When you stop to enjoy a view, you no longer have the effort of walking to keep you warm.

Sel and the Historic Turf House

Sel adds a human story to the S2 loop. Its old turf house shows a different way of living in this landscape, where homes had to contend with Iceland’s climate.

The house stands alongside the route’s waterfalls and viewpoint. It gives the walk a cultural stop as well as a series of natural sights.

Allow a little time here rather than treating Sel as a name on a trail map. Look at the house and consider how different daily life was from a short visit by car.

The full loop takes in Sel, Sjónarsker, and Svartifoss over 5.8 kilometers. That combination makes it a useful introduction to Skaftafell for travelers who have half a day to spare.

Walk Toward Skaftafellsjökull

A separate marked trail leads toward a viewpoint for Skaftafellsjökull. The outlet glacier gives you a close look at ice flowing out from the much larger Vatnajökull ice cap.

This is a land walk to a view. It is not permission to continue onto the ice, even if the glacier looks close enough to reach.

The national park’s page on Skaftafell hiking trails describes routes to both Svartifoss and Skaftafellsjökull, as well as visitor services and access from Route 1.

If you have already walked the S2 loop, choose the glacier viewpoint as an extra outing only if you still have enough time and energy. The two walks offer different rewards.

Land Walks and Guided Glacier Trips Are Different

A marked path to a waterfall is not the same as a walk across glacier ice. The surface, equipment, and risks are different, even when both outings start near Skaftafell.

For a glacier excursion, use a certified glacier guide. Guided trips can include crampons, a helmet, a harness, and an ice axe, depending on the outing.

Some trips also include transport between the meeting point and the glacier edge. One guided Falljökull outing, for example, uses a 4x4 bus from Skaftafell Base Camp.

Meeting places are not interchangeable. Skaftafell, Freysnes, and Jökulsárlón are separate departure areas, so choose accommodation and driving plans around the exact meeting point on your booking.

Why You Should Not Walk onto Ice Alone

Glacier ice can contain crevasses, which are deep cracks in the ice. Snow or changing ice can make hazards difficult for an untrained visitor to recognize.

A glacier also changes over time. A route that looks straightforward from a distance may contain dangerous ground that is not obvious from the edge.

The glacier safety advice from Iceland’s search and rescue organization explains crevasse hazards and recommends hiring a certified glacier guide.

Stay on the marked land trail when walking independently. If stepping onto ice is the goal, book a guided excursion with the equipment and instruction needed for that separate activity.

Choose a Trail and Dress for the Weather

Start with the time you have. The S2 loop is 5.8 kilometers and takes about two hours of walking, while photographs and longer breaks will add to the outing.

Match the climb to your fitness as well. An ascent of approximately 250 meters is a substantial part of the walk, even though the distance may look modest on a map.

Wear suitable walking shoes or boots and carry warm layers. Rain protection is useful in Iceland, where a comfortable start does not guarantee a dry finish.

For a guided glacier trip, read the equipment requirements carefully. Some trips require hiking boots with ankle support and can refuse participation if your footwear is unsuitable.

Parking Fees and Practical Costs

Skaftafell charges an area or parking fee. The listed rate for a passenger car with up to five seats is 1,110 Icelandic króna, commonly written as ISK.

For a vehicle with six to nine seats, the listed charge is 1,440 ISK. The listed motorcycle rate is 450 ISK.

The fee is valid until midnight on the date paid. That matters if your travel plans involve arriving late in the day rather than spending a full day at the site.

The national park also lists an area or parking fee at Jökulsárlón. Keep parking costs separate from any guided trip price, since a tour does not always include every local charge.

Getting to Skaftafell on Route 1

 

Skaftafell is reached from Route 1, Iceland’s Ring Road. The road makes the area a natural stop on a journey along the south and southeast coast.

Do not plan the drive as if every kilometer will be spent at full speed. Weather, road conditions, short winter daylight, and sightseeing stops can all add time.

For a sense of scale, the drive from Reykjavík to Höfn via Route 1 is approximately 450 kilometers, or 280 miles. The regional estimate is six hours without stops.

The Ring Road was completed in 1974, when the final bridge across the Skeiðará was finished. The nearby black outwash plain, Skeiðarársandur, covers approximately 1,300 square kilometers.

Add Jökulsárlón and Diamond Beach

Jökulsárlón is a glacier lagoon beside Route 1 and a possible addition to a longer southeast Iceland trip. Ice from Breiðamerkurjökull floats across the lagoon and through its outlet toward the Atlantic.

Some pieces wash onto the black sand at Diamond Beach. Their pale ice stands out against the dark shore, though the amount and shape of ice on the beach differ from day to day.

Stay well back from the surf and watch the sea. Unexpected waves can reach far up the beach, and ice in the water or surf zone can break or overturn.

Do not climb on lagoon ice or on ice in the water. Seals can sometimes be seen in the lagoon, so leave time to look from a safe position on land.

Short Walks Near Hoffellsjökull

Travelers continuing toward the Höfn area can add a short walk at Hoffellsjökull. The O1 route starts at the glacier’s parking area and leads to a viewpoint.

The O1 walk is 0.5 kilometers round trip, or about a third of a mile. The estimated walking time is ten minutes.

For a longer stretch of the legs, the nearby Húsberg loop covers 1.5 kilometers and takes approximately 30 minutes. It is still a short outing compared with Skaftafell’s S2 circuit.

Treat Hoffellsjökull as a separate stop, not as another trail from the Skaftafell visitor center. It fits best into a journey continuing east toward Höfn.

A Soak at Hoffell Geothermal Hot Springs

Hoffell Geothermal Hot Springs offers four hot tubs and a different way to rest after a day of walking. It is another possible stop for travelers heading toward the Höfn area.

The hot water source was discovered in 2000. At first, the water was pumped into a small fish tub, a modest beginning for the present bathing stop.

The listed admission is 3,500 ISK for adults and 800 ISK for children. The last booking is listed for 7 p.m.

Published hours are noon to 9 p.m. in October, November, and February through April, and 10 a.m. to 9 p.m. from May through September. Admission and hours vary by season.

Continue to Höfn

Höfn is a useful eastern stop on a southeast Iceland road trip. It gives travelers a place to plan the next stage rather than trying to fit every sight into one day.

The regional driving estimate from Höfn to Jökulsárlón is 45 minutes in good conditions. Add time for stops and for any booked activity with a fixed meeting time.

Langoustine is a local food specialty worth looking for in Höfn. It offers a change from the snacks and packed lunches that often accompany a long day on the road.

Family-run Humarhöfnin opened during the town’s 2007 Lobster Festival. The family commissioned a wooden langoustine goddess named Langastína and holds a spring feast in her honor.

A Flexible Day at Skaftafell

Begin at Skaftafellsstofa Visitor Center and ask about the route you intend to walk. Use the answer to decide whether the 5.8-kilometer S2 loop suits the day.

Walk the loop at a pace that leaves room for Hundafoss, Magnúsarfoss, Svartifoss, Sjónarsker, and Sel. The two-hour estimate is a starting point, not an allowance for a long stop at every sight.

If time and energy allow, add the marked walk toward the Skaftafellsjökull viewpoint. Remember that looking at the glacier from a land trail is separate from walking on its ice.

Leave Jökulsárlón, Diamond Beach, and Hoffell as optional additions. They can enrich a longer itinerary, but there is no need to squeeze all three into the same day as Skaftafell.

Final Planning Checklist

Before leaving home, note the visitor center’s seasonal hours and the parking charge for your vehicle. At Skaftafell, use local advice to settle on a route that matches the conditions.

Pack suitable footwear, warm layers, rain protection, water, and food for the time you expect to spend out. The S2 loop includes about 250 meters of climbing.

Keep your glacier plans clear. A marked land trail can take you to a viewpoint, but any excursion onto glacier ice calls for a certified guide and appropriate equipment.

If you book a guided trip, write down the exact meeting place. A departure from Freysnes or Jökulsárlón is not the same as a departure from Skaftafell.

 

 

Conclusion: Give the Landscape Room

Skaftafell rewards a simple plan. Start at the visitor center, choose a trail that fits your day, and give the sights enough time. On the S2 loop, 5.8 kilometers bring together three waterfalls, the viewpoint at Sjónarsker, and the old turf house at Sel.

The walk toward Skaftafellsjökull offers another view of the park, this time with glacier ice as the focus. Enjoy that view from the land trail. For a closer experience on the ice, join a certified guide.

Beyond Skaftafell, the southeast coast offers more than one day can comfortably hold. Jökulsárlón and Diamond Beach make a memorable pair, while Hoffellsjökull, the hot tubs at Hoffell, and Höfn suit a longer journey east. Leave space between stops. The best part of the trip may be the time you spend standing still, looking at black rock, moving water, or ice shaped over many years.
